Etched Lacquerware

Etched Lacquerware

Etched Lacquerware, also known as Kanyut Yun, is one of the most exquisite forms of Myanmar traditional art. It is characterized by its incredibly detailed patterns that are hand-engraved onto the lacquer surface and then filled with vibrant colors. The most famous sub-type is "Za-Yun," known for its lace-like, delicate craftsmanship.

Step-by-Step Process of Etched Lacquerware

The process begins by using a fine iron stylus to engrave intricate designs onto the hardened black resin base. Next, natural pigments such as cinnabar (red) or orpiment (yellow) are inlaid into these grooves. Finally, the surface is polished with water and stone to remove excess paint, leaving behind only the colorful, detailed patterns embedded in the lines.

Where to find Each Lacquerware?

The primary hub for authentic Etched Lacquerware is Bagan, the ancient city of Myanmar, where master artisans have preserved this craft for generations. Visitors can find high-quality pieces in local workshops and galleries throughout Bagan and Nyaung-U, as well as in traditional craft souvenir shops in major cities like Mandalay and Yangon.

Gold Leaft Lacquerware

History and Cultural Significance

Dating back to the Bagan era, Shwe-zawa is considered the "King of Lacquerware." Historically, it was a royal craft used to decorate palace pillars, monastic chests (Pitaka boxes), and ceremonial offerings for the King. The name "Shwe-zawa" itself refers to the intricate gold floral patterns that symbolize purity and royalty in Myanmar culture.

The Detailed Technical Process

1.The Canvas

After the base lacquerware is made (from bamboo or wood), it undergoes at least 7 layers of raw lacquer coating. Each layer is dried and polished until it achieves a "Piano Black" high-gloss finish.

2.Painting the "Orpiment"

The artisan uses a fine brush made of squirrel hair to paint the design. The paint is a special mixture of Hany-tha-pya (cinnabar) and a water-soluble glue. Importantly, the artisan must paint the negative space or the specific lines where the gold is intended to remain.

3.Gilding - The Gold Touch

While the adhesive is still slightly tacky, genuine 24-karat gold leaves (Shwe-si) are placed over the entire surface. The artisan then uses a soft cotton ball to gently press the gold leaf into every fine line of the painted design.

4.The Reveal - Water Washing

This is the most magical part. The piece is washed with water. The gold leaf on the unpainted parts is washed away, leaving behind the brilliant gold patterns. This process requires a very steady hand; otherwise, the delicate gold lines could be damaged.

Artistic Designs and Themes

Common themes in Shwe-zawa include the Twelve Zodiac Signs, scenes from the Jataka tales (stories of the Buddha), and traditional patterns like A-kanut (foliage) and Zinn-me (geometric flowers). The complexity of the design determines the final price of the product.

Where and How to Acquire

1.Locations: For premium collectors, the Bagan House Lacquerware Workshop and Tun Handcrafted Lacquerware in Bagan are world-renowned for Shwe-zawa. In Yangon, specialized boutiques near the Shwedagon Pagoda also offer museum-quality pieces.

2.Maintenance: Since real gold is used, these pieces should never be cleaned with harsh chemicals. A soft, dry cloth is sufficient to maintain the luster for decades.

Glass Mosaic and Gilded Lacquerware

The Crown Jewel of Myanmar Lacquerware

"Glass Mosaic and Gilded Lacquerware" (Hman-zi-shwe-cha)is widely considered the most magnificent and grandest form of Myanmar’s lacquerware traditions. Historically, this exquisite craftsmanship was primarily reserved for royal regalia and sacred religious artifacts. Far more intricate than standard lacquerware, it involves a series of complex, meticulous steps. Below, I will provide a detailed explanation of the traditional techniques used to create these masterpieces.

Step-by-Step Process of "Glass Mosaic and Gilded Lacquerware"

Glass Mosaic and Gilded Lacquerware is the art of decorating lacquerware or woodenobjects by inlaying glass pieces and applying gold leaf using a mixture called "Thayo" (a paste made of lacquer and ash or sawdust).

1.Preparing the Base
2.Designingwith Thayo (Sculpting the Patterns)
3.GlassInlaying
4.Gold Gilding
5.Revealing the Glass

1.Preparing the Base

The first step involves preparing the base, which is usually made of bamboo or wood. It is coated with multiple layers of raw lacquer and sanded until the surface is perfectly smooth. This stage alone can take several days to complete.

2.Designing with Thayo

This is the most critical stage of the process:

  • Thayo is created by mixing lacquer with turmeric or ash until it reaches a dough-like consistency, then rolled into fine, thin strings.

  • These strings are applied one by one onto the base to form intricate patterns such as Kanote (traditional Burmese scrolls), floral motifs, or geometric designs.

  • Specific gaps are carefully left open within the design where the glass pieces will later be placed.

3.Glass Inlaying

  • Before the Thayo strings dry,small pieces of glass (Hman-zi) are meticulously inserted into the pre-planned gaps.
  • Traditionally, special glass pieces with silver or colored backing were used to provide a vibrant, reflective depth.

4.Gold Gilding

  • Once theinlaying is complete, the entire object is coated with a layer of clear lacquer, known as Shwe-thayo.
  • When the lacquer becomes tacky (slightly sticky), genuine gold leaf is applied piece by piece over the entire surface.
  • At this stage, the entire object appears completely covered in brilliant gold.

5.Revealing the Glass

  • After the gilding is done, the glass pieces are usually covered by the gold leaf.
  • Using a soft cloth or cotton bud dipped in water, the artist carefully wipes the gold off the surface of the glass pieces.
  • This reveals the sparkling glass shining through the gold background. This final reveal is known as "Hman Phaw Chin."

where to acquire

  • Bagan (Cultural Hub): Large galleries in Bagan like Ever Stand Lacquerware focus on preserving these traditional royal designs.
  • Mandalay (Artisan District): Mandalay is the best place to find religious artifacts and large-scale Glass Mosaic and Gilded Lacquerware items, especially in the craft workshops near the Mahamuni Pagoda.

Plain Lacquerware

Introduction and Heritage

Plain Lacquerware, or Thoke-Yun, represents the most fundamental yet essential form of Myanmar's lacquer tradition. While other styles focus on surface decoration, Thoke-Yun celebrates the raw beauty of natural resin and the structural integrity of the piece. Historically, it has been the "people's lacquerware," serving as a bridge between royal art and everyday utility. From the humble monks' bowls to family dining sets, Thoke-Yun is a testament to the durability and timelessness of Myanmar craftsmanship.

The Master Crafting Process

1.Slecting the Base

The foundation is usually made of finely split bamboo (Tin-wa) or wood. For premium quality, horsehair is woven into the bamboo to create flexibility, allowing the piece to bend without cracking.

2.Layering with thi-si

The item is coated with several layers of pure black lacquer. Each layer must be thoroughly dried in a dark, humid underground cellar (Myay-taik) for about a week. This slow drying process is crucial for the lacquer’s hardness.

3.Sanding and polishing

After every coating cycle, the surface is polished using water and fine stone or sandpaper. This removes any imperfections, ensuring that the final product is as smooth as glass. For Thoke-Yun, the quality is judged by this smoothness.

4.Final Pigmentation

The final layers define the variant. A-net-htae (Blackware) uses pure lacquer, while A-ni-htae (Redware) is achieved by mixing lacquer with high-grade cinnabar.

Durability and Modern Value

Thoke-Yun is famous for being incredibly light, waterproof, and resistant to heat and acid. In today world, it is highly valued by eco-conscious consumers as a sustainable, chemical-free alternative to plastic and synthetic tableware.Its minimalist look fits perfectly with modern home decor.

Where to find Plain Lacquerware?

Kyaukka Village,Monywa

Kyaukka village, located near Monywa, is the most famous hub for a specific type of plain lacquerware known as "Kyaukka Yun." These are typically deep red (A-ni-htae) and are known for their sturdiness and large sizes, such as huge circular trays (Daung-lan) and storage containers.

Bagan

While Bagan is famous for artistic engraving, it also produces high-quality black and red plain lacquerware. In villages like Myinkaba and Phwa-saw, you can find polished plain sets that focus on elegance and smoothness, often used in modern home decor and high-end dining

Relief Molded Lacquerware

Overview and Visual Style

Relief Molded Lacquerware, often called Thayo-Pan or Yun-lone-htwin, is a unique technique where the patterns are raised from the surface to create a three-dimensional effect. Unlike etched lacquer, this style uses a thick lacquer paste to mold figures, flowers, and mythical creatures, making the artwork physically stand out. It is highly valued for its texture and the lifelike quality of its designs.

The Sculpting peocess

1.preparing the "Thayo"Paste

The artisan creates a thick, clay-like paste by mixing raw lacquer (Thit-si) with fine ash (from cow bone or teak wood) or sawdust. This mixture is flexible when wet but becomes rock-hard when dry.

2.Hand-Molding the Designs

Small amounts of Thayo are rolled into thin threads or tiny balls. The artisan then carefully applies these onto the lacquer surface to build up figures layer by layer. For complex designs like lions or dragons, specific parts are molded separately and then attached to the main piece.

Carving the Details

While the Thayo is still semi-firm, the artisan uses small wooden or metal tools to carve fine details like scales on a dragon or petals on a flower. This requires a very steady hand and a keen eye for anatomy and symmetry.

Hardening and Coating

Once the molding is complete, the piece is dried thoroughly. It is then coated with a final layer of high-quality lacquer to seal the design. Sometimes, gold leaf is applied over the relief to create a "Gilded Relief" effect.

Common Themes and Usage

Relief molded lacquerware is often seen on decorative items like large wall plaques, ornamental vases, and ceremonial boxes (Kun-it). The themes usually include Myanmar folklore, mythical creatures like the Garuda or Naga, and intricate floral scrolls.

where to find?

Bagan Workshops: Expert workshops in Bagan (such as U Ba Nyein) are the best places to find master-level relief work.
In Mandalay,Arts and Craft Centers are specialize art centers often sell these.

Gem-studded Lacquerware

Overview and Royal Elegance

Gem-studded Lacquerware is the ultimate expression of luxury in Myanmar traditional arts. It is an evolution of the glass mosaic technique, where instead of glass, authentic precious and semi-precious stones are used. Historically, these masterpieces were crafted exclusively for royalty and high-ranking nobility, often used in royal regalia, ceremonial boxes, and as offerings in the most sacred temples.

The Master Crafting Process

1.Creating tne Ornate Base

The foundation is usually a high-grade wooden or thick lacquer base. Like the Relief Molded style, intricate patterns are first built up using Thayo (lacquer paste) to create "beds" or "sockets" for the gemstones.

2.Selection and Setting of Gems

Authentic Myanmar gems such as rubies, sapphires, jade, and pearls, along with semi-precious stones like quartz and garnet, are selected. Each stone is meticulously hand-set into the wet lacquer paste. For royal pieces, the "Navaratna" (nine gems) are often used to symbolize power and protection.

3.Gold Leaf Gilding

Once the stones are securely set, the surrounding patterns and the entire surface are gilded with 24-karat gold leaf (*Shwe-si*). The contrast between the sparkling colorful gems and the deep golden background creates a breathtaking aesthetic.

4.Final polishing

The stones are carefully cleaned and polished to remove any stray lacquer or gold leaf, ensuring each gem shines with its natural brilliance.

Artistic Value and Significance

These items are more than just lacquerware;they are considered family heirlooms and spiritual artifacts. Common items include Kun-it (betel nut boxes), ceremonial bowls, and Buddha shrines. They represent the peak of Myanmar’s craftsmanship, blending mineral wealth with artistic soul.

Where to Find?

Premium Art Galleries: Gem-studded lacquerware is rare and usually sold in high-end galleries in Yangon (near major hotels) and Bagan.
Special Orders: Most authentic pieces are custom-made by master artisans in Bagan or Mandalay for collectors and museum displays.
